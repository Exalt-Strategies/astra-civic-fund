The temple that volunteers built

Before it was one of the largest Hindu temples in the country, the Sri Siva Vishnu Temple was an idea shared among a few friends. Nearly fifty years later, it still runs the way it started: on volunteers, organized into committees.

Astra StoriesLanham, MarylandOctober 21, 2026

Sri Siva Vishnu Temple Governed by a Board of Trustees and a Governance Board Lanham, Maryland · ssvt.org

The temple's own history begins in 1976, among a few friends who believed the nation's capital should have a Hindu temple. By 1980 they had formed the Sri Siva Vishnu Temple Trust. In 1984 the trust bought a plot of land with a small house in suburban Washington, and the first shrines were installed inside it.

What stands on that land today is one of the largest Hindu temples in the United States. Construction began in 1988 under the temple architect Dr. Ganapati Sthapati. In 1990, according to the temple, 1,400 people came to see the first shrines consecrated in the new building. The Rajagopuram and the Vasantha Mandapam followed in 2002, along with an auditorium, a dining hall, and new shrines as the congregation grew.

The building is the part people see. What keeps it running is how the community organized itself to build it, and how it still governs itself today.

Built to outlast its founders

The temple's founding goals, as it describes them, were written for a generation of immigrants: to unite Hindus in America, "individuals diverse in their customs but unified in their devotion and values," and to offer a place where Hindu culture could thrive "among immigrants, their children and their children's children."

That last phrase explains a lot about how the temple is set up. Authority does not rest with one founder or one family. A Governance Board of trustees emeriti and current board representatives sits above a sixteen-member Board of Trustees, currently chaired by Thenappan Meyyappan. An executive committee covers operations, human resources and policy, the treasury, and the board's records. The temple publishes its bylaws and an operations manual, and it maintains an Office of the Ombudsperson.

For the long term, the temple set up an endowment with two funds. One supports education and culture. The other provides financial security and covers long-term capital needs. A fiduciary committee that includes two independent financial specialists oversees how the endowment is invested. That kind of planning is what lets an institution outlive the people who started it.

Where the volunteers work

Beneath the board, the temple runs on a dozen standing committees and seven task forces, each with a coordinator and most with a deputy. Some of the work is sacred and some is ordinary. The Flowers Committee prepares garlands for every deity. The Food Committee serves devotees on weekends, holidays and festival days, and schedules the volunteers who do it. An IT committee runs everything from the front-desk phones and payment systems to the newsletter and social media. Task forces count the hundi collections, keep the devotee database, tend the gardens, manage insurance, and publish the monthly newsletter.

Other committees look outward. The Education Committee's stated goals include teaching children of Hindu roots about their heritage, helping parents and adults "better represent the Hindu community in interfaith settings," and giving people from non-Indian backgrounds a chance to learn about Hindu culture. The Cultural Committee gives young artists in the region a stage. There is a Senior Citizens Group, and a Youth and Community Service committee, called Aakaar, whose coordinator seat is listed as open.

The temple's general council lists more than eighty people across trustees, trustees emeriti, donors and coordinators. In the temple's own words, it is today "the enterprise of dedicated volunteers, administrative staff and eleven priests."

How it sees its role

The temple's published vision statement says it began as a way of bringing Hindus together but has increasingly become "a place that brings together Hindu customs and heritage with American values of community and volunteerism."

The same statement describes service beyond the congregation. It mentions a Children's Fund for youth and education programs, and a volunteer network called Mitra Mandala, or Circle of Friends, to support families coping with illness, grief and aging. It also describes service through local charities such as soup kitchens and shelters. It ends with the next generation: just as the temple began in the minds of a few recent immigrants, "its future lies in the hands of younger generation."

The next twelve years

Tradition calls for a temple to be re-consecrated every twelve years, with repairs and renovations completed beforehand. The Sri Siva Vishnu Temple is preparing for its next re-consecration, the Jeernodharana Kumbhabhishekam in 2027, which it calls a project of "revitalization and restoration." After twenty years of use, the roof of the prakaram, the covered walkway around the shrines, needs to be replaced, and the temple is raising funds for that work too.

In its appeal to devotees, the temple describes the renovation as more than upkeep: it is "building a vital bridge that connects our children and future generations" to their heritage.

Why we are telling this story

Astra's argument is that a community's standing comes from the institutions it builds, not only from its numbers. Long before anyone called it civic infrastructure, a few families in suburban Maryland built some: a trust, bylaws, a board, an endowment, and a volunteer committee for nearly every task. Any Indian American organization that hopes to outlast its founders can learn from it.
